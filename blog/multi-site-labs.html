Running several testing labs? 6 places where sites drift apart, and how to keep them on one process

Vaibhav Singh, founder · 12 November 2026 · 6 min read

A second or third lab site brings more capacity and closer clients. It also brings a quieter problem: each site slowly develops its own way of working. Registers look different, tests are named differently, and the accreditation mark ends up wherever the local template puts it. Here are six places where multi-site labs drift apart, and how LabTessa keeps every site on the same process.

1Each site has its own test list

The central lab calls it "Aflatoxin B1, LC-MS/MS". A branch calls it "Aflatoxin (B1)" and charges a different price. Clients who send samples to two sites get two kinds of report, and nobody can compare work between sites.

TodayEvery site keeps its own spreadsheet of tests, methods and prices.
With LabTessaWe load your catalogue into every site during setup, so all sites start from the same tests, methods and units. Requests are matched only to tests in that catalogue.

2Sample numbers that clash between sites

When two sites both number their samples from 001, a report, a retest or a complaint can be traced to the wrong sample. It usually comes to light at the worst possible moment, during an audit or a client dispute.

TodayEach site runs its own register and its own numbering.
With LabTessaEach site is its own lab in LabTessa, with a short site code built into every sample code. A sample from one site can never be mistaken for one from another.

3One report template, different accreditation scopes

Sites are usually accredited separately, and their scopes differ. A test that is in scope at the central lab may not be at a branch. If every site uses the same report template, the NABL or ISO/IEC 17025 mark ends up on tests it shouldn't cover.

TodayThe same template and the same mark, whichever site did the test.
With LabTessaScope is recorded for each site, by test and matrix. A report marks only the tests that are inside that site's own scope.

4Limits and calculations copied from site to site

Limits and formulas travel between sites as printouts and spreadsheet copies. When a regulation changes, one site updates and another doesn't, and two labs in the same group judge the same result differently.

TodayEach site keeps its own copy, updated whenever someone remembers.
With LabTessaEach limit is stored with its source, so every report shows what a result was checked against. Calculated tests use the formula and factors entered once, and work the same way every time.

5Review discipline that depends on the site

At a busy branch with a small team, the analyst who enters a result often releases the report too. Your central quality team has no easy way to see whether review really happened.

TodayEach site follows its own habits, and paper signatures are the only evidence.
With LabTessaSecond-person approval works the same at every site: whoever entered a result can't approve it. Results are write-once, a corrected report says which one it replaces, and every step is recorded with who did it and when.

6Staff who work across sites

Quality heads, technical managers and relief analysts move between sites. With separate systems, they end up with separate logins, separate files and no clean line between one site's data and another's.

TodayA separate login or spreadsheet for each site, and data mixed in shared folders.
With LabTessaOne person can be a member of several sites, with a role at each, and chooses which site to work in. Each site's samples, clients and reports stay separate, enforced in the database itself, not just by the screens.
Also at every site: optional AI intake that drafts samples and tests from a client's email or PDF (off until a lab admin turns it on), QR-verified reports and COAs that show whether a report is genuine without revealing client data, nightly encrypted backups, and data hosted in India. Your accreditation body assesses each lab, not the software, but these features are designed to make every site's assessment simpler.
